## Note
**Spouses as partners (p. 15).** When spouses jointly own and run a business and split its profits and losses, they may be partners in a partnership. This can be so with or without a formal partnership agreement. The page points to Pub. 541 for more details. The partnership counts as the employer of any employees and is liable for employment taxes due on the wages it pays them.

**Exception—qualified joint venture (pp. 15–16).** For tax years beginning after 2006, the Small Business and Work Opportunity Tax Act of 2007 (P.L. 110-28) lets a "qualified joint venture" elect out of partnership treatment for federal tax purposes; its only members must be spouses filing a joint income tax return. A qualified joint venture carries on a trade or business in which:
- Spouses who file a joint income tax return are the venture's only members.
- Each spouse materially participates in the trade or business (the page refers to Material participation in the instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040), line G); owning property jointly is not enough.
- Each spouse elects not to be treated as a partnership.
- The two spouses co-own the business, and it is not held in the name of a state law entity such as a partnership or limited liability company (LLC).

To make the election, every item of income, gain, loss, deduction and credit must be split between the spouses according to each one's interest in the venture, and each spouse reports their share as a sole proprietor on a separate Schedule C (Form 1040) or Schedule F (Form 1040) (p. 16). Each spouse must in addition file a separate Schedule SE (Form 1040) to pay self-employment taxes, as applicable. For American Samoa, Guam, the CNMI, the USVI and Puerto Rico, the page refers to the Instructions for Form 1040-SS.

Spouses under the qualified joint venture rules are treated as sole proprietors for federal tax purposes, and generally need no EIN. When the venture owes employment taxes, either spouse may report and pay them, on the wages paid to the employees, under the EIN of that spouse's sole proprietorship. Generally, filing as a qualified joint venture will not raise the spouses' total tax on their joint income tax return. It does give each spouse credit for the social security earnings that retirement benefits are based on, and for Medicare coverage, without a partnership return being filed (p. 16).

If your spouse is your employee rather than your partner, the page refers to One spouse employed by another in section 3. For more on qualified joint ventures it points to IRS.gov/QJV (p. 16).

**Exception—community income (p. 16).** Spouses who wholly own an unincorporated business as community property under the community property laws of a state, foreign country or U.S. territory can treat it as a sole proprietorship (of the spouse who carried on the business), or as a partnership. They may still elect to be taxed as a qualified joint venture rather than as a partnership.
