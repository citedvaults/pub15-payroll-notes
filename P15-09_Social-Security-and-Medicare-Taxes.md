## Note
**FICA (p. 29).** The Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) sets up a federal system of old-age, survivors, disability and hospital insurance. The social security tax finances the old-age, survivors and disability part, and the Medicare tax finances the hospital insurance part. The two taxes are reported separately. Generally, you must withhold social security and Medicare taxes from employees' wages and pay the employer share. Certain types of wages and compensation are not subject to these taxes, and the page refers to section 5 and section 15 for details. Generally, employee wages are subject to them whatever the employee's age and whether or not the employee receives social security benefits. For an employee who reported tips, the page refers to section 6.

**The $150 test or the $2,500 test for farmwork (p. 29).** All cash wages you pay an employee during the year for farmwork are subject to social security and Medicare taxes and to federal income tax withholding if either of two tests is met:
- You pay the employee cash wages of $150 or more in a year for farmwork (all cash wages count, whether paid on a time, piecework or other basis). The $150 test applies separately to each farmworker you employ, each member of a family of workers being treated separately. Wages paid by other employers are not counted.
- Your total payments for farmwork (cash and noncash) to all your employees are $2,500 or more during the year.

**Exceptions (p. 29).** A seasonal farmworker's annual cash wages of less than $150 from you are not subject to social security and Medicare taxes or federal income tax withholding, even if you pay $2,500 or more to all your farmworkers. These wages do count toward the $2,500 test when determining whether other farmworkers' wages are subject to social security and Medicare taxes. A seasonal farmworker meets all four of these descriptions:
- They are employed in agriculture as a hand-harvest laborer.
- They are paid piece rates in an operation that is usually paid on a piece-rate basis in the region of employment.
- They commute daily from a permanent home to the farm.
- They had been employed in agriculture less than 13 weeks in the preceding calendar year.

Social security and Medicare taxes also don't apply to wages paid to a child under 18 who works on a farm that is a sole proprietorship, or a partnership in which each partner is a parent of a child. Those wages likewise count toward the $2,500 test when determining whether other farmworkers' wages are subject to those taxes.

**Deducting the tax (p. 29).** The employee tax is deducted from each wage payment. If you are unsure whether a farmworker's wages for the year will be taxable, you may either deduct the tax as you make payments or wait until the $2,500 test or the $150 test has been met.

**Tax rates and the social security wage base limit (p. 29).** Social security and Medicare taxes have different rates, and only social security tax has a wage base limit, the maximum wage subject to the tax for the year. Withholding for each tax is figured by multiplying each payment by the employee tax rate. For 2026 the social security tax rate is 6.2% (the amount withheld) for the employer and 6.2% for the employee (12.4% total), and the social security wage base limit is $184,500. The Medicare tax rate is 1.45% (the amount withheld) for the employee and 1.45% for the employer (2.9% total). Medicare tax has no wage base limit, so all covered wages are subject to it.

**Additional Medicare Tax withholding (pp. 29–30).** On top of withholding Medicare tax at 1.45%, you must withhold a 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax from wages you pay an employee in excess of $200,000 in a calendar year. Withholding must begin in the pay period in which you pay wages over $200,000 to the employee and continue each pay period until the end of the calendar year. Only the employee bears Additional Medicare Tax, and there is no employer share. All wages subject to Medicare tax are subject to Additional Medicare Tax withholding when paid in excess of the $200,000 withholding threshold (p. 30). For what wages are subject to Medicare tax the page refers to section 15, and it points to IRS.gov/ADMTfaqs for more on Additional Medicare Tax (p. 30).

**Successor employer (p. 30).** If corporate acquisitions meet certain requirements, wages the predecessor paid are treated as paid by the successor for applying the social security wage base limit and the Additional Medicare Tax withholding threshold ($200,000 in a calendar year). Whether you should file Schedule D (Form 941) is determined by reviewing the Instructions for Schedule D (Form 941). The page refers to Regulations section 31.3121(a)(1)-1(b) and to Revenue Procedure 2004-53, 2004-34 I.R.B. 320.

Example: Early in 2026 you bought all the assets of a plumbing business from Adam Black. Julian Gold had been employed by Adam and had received $2,000 in wages before the purchase date, and he continued working for you. Social security tax applies to the first $182,500 ($184,500 minus $2,000) of the wages you pay Julian. Medicare tax is due on all the wages you pay him in the calendar year. The $2,000 he received while employed by Adam should be counted in determining whether his wages exceed the $200,000 threshold for Additional Medicare Tax withholding.

**Employee's portion of taxes paid by employer (p. 30).** When you pay an employee's social security and Medicare taxes instead of deducting them from the employee's pay, the amount of those payments must be included in the employee's wages for social security and Medicare taxes. That increase in the wage payment is itself subject to employee social security and Medicare taxes, which again increases the additional taxes you must pay. The page refers to Revenue Ruling 86-14 and Pub. 15-A.

**Household and agricultural employers (p. 30).** For a household or agricultural employee, you may pay the employee's share of social security and Medicare taxes without withholding it from the employee's wages. If you don't withhold the taxes, you must still pay them on the employee's behalf. The employee social security and Medicare taxes you pay count as additional income to the employee. Put that income in box 1 of the employee's Form W-2 (box 7 of Form 499R-2/W-2PR), but leave it out of social security and Medicare wages and out of boxes 3 and 5 (boxes 20 and 22 of Form 499R-2/W-2PR). It is not wages for FUTA tax purposes either. For non-household and non-agricultural employees different rules apply to an employer's payment of these taxes; the page refers to section 7 of Pub. 15-A.

Example: Gavrielle runs a small family fruit farm and hires day laborers in the picking season. Instead of deducting the employees' share of social security and Medicare taxes from their pay, Gavrielle pays it on their behalf. When preparing the employees' Forms W-2, Gavrielle adds each employee's share of the taxes paid to the employee's wage income in box 1, and leaves it out of box 3 (social security wages) and box 5 (Medicare wages and tips). For 2026, Dan was paid $1,000 during the year. Gavrielle enters $1,076.50 in box 1 of Dan's Form W-2 ($1,000 wages plus $76.50 of social security and Medicare taxes paid for him) and $1,000.00 in boxes 3 and 5.

**Sick pay payments (p. 30).** Most sick pay payments, those made by third parties such as insurance companies included, are subject to social security and Medicare taxes. For details on third-party payers of sick pay the page refers to Pub. 15-A.

**Motion picture project employers (p. 30).** A motion picture project employer's payments to a motion picture project worker during a calendar year are all subject to one social security tax wage base limit ($184,500 for 2026) and one FUTA tax wage base limit ($7,000 for 2026), whether or not the worker is a common-law employee of multiple clients of the employer. The page refers to section 3512 for more information, including the definitions of a "motion picture project employer" and a "motion picture project worker."

**Withholding social security and Medicare taxes on nonresident alien employees (p. 30).** In general, wages paid to nonresident alien employees are treated for social security and Medicare tax withholding as they would be for a U.S. citizen or resident alien. The page refers to Pub. 515 for exceptions. One exception covers foreign agricultural workers on H-2A visas, who are exempt from social security and Medicare taxes; the page refers to Compensation paid to H-2A visa holders in section 5.

**International social security agreements (p. 30).** The United States has social security agreements, also called totalization agreements, with many countries; they eliminate dual social security coverage and taxation. Compensation subject to social security and Medicare taxes may be exempt under one of them. More information and a list of agreement countries are available from the SSA at SSA.gov/international, and the page refers to Pub. 519.

**Religious exemption (pp. 30–31).** Members of a recognized religious sect opposed to insurance can claim an exemption from social security and Medicare taxes. It is available only if the employee and the employer are both members of the sect. The page refers to Pub. 517, Social Security and Other Information for Members of the Clergy and Religious Workers (p. 31).

**Foreign persons treated as American employers (p. 31).** Under section 3121(z), a foreign person is generally treated as an American employer when paying FICA taxes on wages to an employee who is a U.S. citizen or resident, if both of these conditions are met:
1. The foreign person is a member of a domestically controlled group of entities.
2. The foreign person's employee performs services in connection with a contract between the U.S. Government (or one of its instrumentalities) and any member of the domestically controlled group of entities.

Ownership of more than 50% constitutes control.
