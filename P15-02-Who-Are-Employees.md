## Note
Section 2 of the publication covers who is an employee: workers who are employees under common law, persons treated as employees or as nonemployees by statute, farmworkers and crew leaders, the treatment of an employee as a nonemployee, and relief and classification programs. Its one part deals with a business that spouses own and operate. The chapter's own text, summarised below, runs from p. 14 to p. 15.

**Definitions (p. 14).** Generally, employees are defined either under common law or under statutes for certain situations. For details on statutory employees and nonemployees the page points to Pub. 15-A.

**Employee status under common law (p. 14).** Generally, someone who performs services for you is your employee when you have the right to control what is done and how it is done, even if you leave the employee free to act. The deciding point is the right to control the details of how the services are performed. For more on deciding whether an individual providing services is an independent contractor or an employee, the page points to Pub. 15-A. People in business for themselves are generally not employees: doctors, lawyers, veterinarians and others in an independent trade in which they offer their services to the public are usually not employees. Corporate officers who work in an incorporated business are employees of the corporation. Where an employer-employee relationship exists, its name doesn't matter, and the employee may be called an agent or independent contractor. How payments are measured or paid, what they are called, and whether the employee works full or part time also don't matter.

**Statutory employees (p. 14).** When someone who works for you is not an employee under the common-law rules, don't withhold federal income tax from their pay, unless backup withholding applies. The persons below may not be common-law employees, yet they are considered employees by statute for social security and Medicare tax purposes if the conditions under Tests, later, are met:
- An agent or commission driver who delivers meat, vegetable, fruit or bakery products, beverages (other than milk), laundry or dry cleaning for someone else.
- A full-time life insurance salesperson who sells primarily for one company.
- A homeworker who works at home or off premises following the guidelines of the person for whom the work is done, with materials or goods furnished by that person and returned to that person or to someone that person designates.
- A traveling or city salesperson (other than an agent or commission driver) who works full time (except for sideline sales activities) for one firm or person, getting orders from customers. Those orders must be for merchandise for resale or for supplies used in the customer's business, and the customers must be retailers, wholesalers, contractors, or operators of hotels, restaurants or other businesses dealing with food or lodging.

**Tests (p. 14).** Social security and Medicare taxes are withheld from a statutory employee's wages if all three tests apply:
1. The service contract states or implies that almost all of the services are to be performed personally by the worker.
2. The worker has little or no investment in the equipment and property used to perform the services apart from any investment in transportation facilities.
3. The services are performed on a continuing basis for the same payer.

Persons in the first group above (agent or commission driver) or the fourth (traveling or city salesperson) are also employees for FUTA tax purposes if tests 1 through 3 are met. Examples of the employer-employee relationship are in Pub. 15-A.

**Statutory nonemployees (p. 14).** By law, direct sellers, qualified real estate agents and certain companion sitters are considered nonemployees. They are generally treated as self-employed for all federal tax purposes, income and employment taxes included. The page points to Pub. 15-A for more information.

**Farmworkers (pp. 14–15).** In general, you are an employer of farmworkers if your employees do any of the following:
- Raise or harvest agricultural or horticultural products on your farm (raising and feeding livestock included).
- Work in connection with operating, managing, conserving, improving or maintaining your farm and its tools and equipment, if the major part of that service is performed on a farm.
- Provide services relating to salvaging timber, or to clearing brush and other debris from land after a hurricane (also known as hurricane labor), if the major part of that service is performed on a farm.
- Handle, process or package an agricultural or horticultural commodity in its unmanufactured state, if you produced over half of the commodity (for a group of up to 20 unincorporated operators, all of it).
- Do work for you related to cotton ginning, turpentine, gum resin products, or the operation and maintenance of irrigation facilities.

Here "farm" covers stock, dairy, poultry, fruit, fur-bearing animal and truck farms, plantations, ranches, nurseries, ranges, orchards, and greenhouses or similar structures used primarily to raise agricultural or horticultural commodities. Reselling activities with no substantial activity of raising agricultural or horticultural commodities, such as a retail store or a greenhouse used primarily for display or storage, are not farmwork. Neither are processing services that change a commodity from its raw or natural state, nor services performed after a commodity has been changed from that state.

**Crew leaders (p. 15).** A crew leader is an employer of farmworkers. A crew leader furnishes and pays workers (on their own behalf or on behalf of the farm operator) to do farmwork for the farm operator. You are a crew leader if you pay the workers (for yourself or for the farm operator) and no written agreement between you and the farm operator says you are their employee. For FUTA tax rules the page refers to section 14. A crew leader is not considered the farm operator's employee for services performed in furnishing farmworkers and as a member of the crew.

**H-2A agricultural workers (p. 15).** H-2A workers are not statutory employees, so on Form W-2 don't check box 13 (Statutory employee).

**Treating employees as nonemployees (p. 15).** Treating an employee as a nonemployee, and so not deducting and withholding social security and Medicare taxes and income tax, generally leaves you liable for those taxes and for the income tax that should have been withheld. You may be able to figure your liability with special section 3509 rates for the employee share of social security and Medicare taxes and for federal income tax withholding; which rates apply depends on whether you filed required Forms 1099. If the tax is paid under section 3509, the employee share of social security tax, Medicare tax and income tax withholding can't be recovered from the employee. Liability for the income tax withholding exists regardless of whether the employee paid income tax on the wages. You still owe the full employer share of social security and Medicare taxes, and the employee remains liable for the employee share of those taxes. The page refers to section 3509 for details and to the Instructions for Form 941-X, Form 943-X and Form 944-X. The section 3509 rates can't be used when you intentionally disregard the requirement to withhold taxes from the employee, or when you withheld income taxes without withholding social security or Medicare tax. Section 3509 is also not available for reclassifying statutory employees.

Where the employer issued required information returns, the section 3509 rates are:
- Social security taxes: the employer rate of 6.2% plus 20% of the 6.2% employee rate, a total of 7.44% of wages.
- Medicare taxes: the employer rate of 1.45% plus 20% of the 1.45% employee rate, a total of 1.74% of wages.
- Additional Medicare Tax: 0.18% (20% of the 0.9% employee rate) of wages subject to Additional Medicare Tax.
- Federal income tax withholding: 1.5% of wages.

Where the employer did not issue required information returns, the section 3509 rates are:
- Social security taxes: the employer rate of 6.2% plus 40% of the 6.2% employee rate, a total of 8.68% of wages.
- Medicare taxes: the employer rate of 1.45% plus 40% of the 1.45% employee rate, a total of 2.03% of wages.
- Additional Medicare Tax: 0.36% (40% of the 0.9% employee rate) of wages subject to Additional Medicare Tax.
- Federal income tax withholding: 3.0% of wages.

**Relief provisions (p. 15).** With a reasonable basis for not treating a worker as an employee, you may be relieved from paying employment taxes for that worker. To get the relief, you must file all required federal tax returns, information returns included, in a way consistent with how you treated the worker. You (or your predecessor) must not have treated any worker in a substantially similar position as an employee for any period beginning after 1977. The page points to Pub. 1976, Do You Qualify for Relief Under Section 530.

**IRS help (p. 15).** To have the IRS determine whether a worker is an employee, file Form SS-8.

**Voluntary Classification Settlement Program (VCSP) (p. 15).** Employers who now treat their workers (or a class or group of workers) as independent contractors or other nonemployees, and want to reclassify them voluntarily as employees for future tax periods, may be eligible for the VCSP if certain requirements are met. Apply by filing Form 8952. The page points to IRS.gov/VCSP for more information.
