## Note
**Wages in general (p. 18).** Wages subject to federal employment taxes generally cover all pay given to an employee for services performed, in cash or in other forms. They include salaries, vacation allowances, bonuses, commissions and taxable fringe benefits, and how the payments are measured or made is irrelevant. A bonus paid for signing or ratifying a contract in connection with establishing an employer-employee relationship is wages subject to social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes and to income tax withholding, and so is an amount paid to an employee for cancelling an employment contract and giving up contract rights. Compensation paid to a former employee for services performed while still employed is likewise wages subject to employment taxes.

**Cash wages paid to farmworkers (p. 18).** Cash wages paid to employees for farmwork are generally subject to social security tax and Medicare tax. Additional Medicare Tax may also have to be withheld, deposited and reported by you, and the page refers to section 9. Wages that are subject to social security and Medicare taxes are subject to federal income tax withholding as well. You owe these taxes to the federal government whether or not you collect them from your employees. For example, if you withhold less than the correct tax from an employee's wages, you are still liable for the full amount. FUTA tax, which you don't withhold and the employee doesn't pay, may also be your liability; the page refers to section 14 for it. Checks, money orders and any kind of money or cash count as cash wages.

**More information (p. 18).** The page refers to section 6 for tips, section 7 for supplemental wages and section 15 for exceptions to the general rules for wages. Pub. 15-A gives additional information on wages (nonqualified deferred compensation among them) and on other compensation. Pub. 15-B covers other forms of compensation, including health savings accounts, group-term life insurance coverage, accident and health benefits and dependent care assistance; achievement awards, adoption assistance and educational assistance; athletic facilities, lodging on your business premises and meals; employee discounts, employee stock options and tuition reduction; employer-provided cell phones, transportation (commuting) benefits and retirement planning services; and de minimis (minimal) benefits, no-additional-cost services and working condition benefits.

**Noncash wages, including commodity wages, paid to farmworkers (pp. 18–19).** Noncash wages are food, lodging, clothing, transportation passes, farm products and other goods or commodities. Paid to farmworkers (commodity wages included), they are not subject to social security tax, Medicare tax or federal income tax withholding. You and your employee can nonetheless agree to have federal income tax withheld on them. If the substance of the transaction is a cash payment, noncash wages (commodity wages included) are treated as cash wages and are then subject to social security tax, Medicare tax and federal income tax withholding. The value of noncash wages goes in box 1 of Form W-2 (box 7 of Form 499R-2/W-2PR) with the cash wages. Noncash wages for farmwork are subject to federal income tax unless a specific exclusion applies. They are not to be shown in box 3 or 5 of Form W-2 (box 20 or 22 of Form 499R-2/W-2PR), except where the substance of the transaction is a cash payment and they are being treated as cash wages.

**Share farmers (p. 19).** Amounts paid to share farmers under share-farming arrangements carry no duty to withhold or pay social security and Medicare taxes. A "share farmer" working for you is not your employee, though the share farmer may owe self-employment tax. In general, share farming means that the farmer and the owner (or tenant) of the land share certain commodity products. The page refers to Regulations section 31.3121(b)(16)-1.

**Compensation paid to H-2A visa holders (p. 19).** For foreign agricultural workers who entered the country on H-2A visas, compensation of $600 or more paid in 2025 ($2,000 or more paid in 2026) is reported in box 1 of Form W-2 (box 7 of Form 499R-2/W-2PR). Neither social security wages (box 3 of Form W-2, box 20 of Form 499R-2/W-2PR) nor Medicare wages (box 5 of Form W-2, box 22 of Form 499R-2/W-2PR) should include it, because compensation for agricultural labor performed in connection with this visa isn't subject to social security and Medicare taxes. H-2A workers aren't statutory employees, so box 13 (Statutory employee) of Form W-2 is not checked. An employer need not withhold federal income tax from this compensation but may do so if the worker asks and the employer agrees; the worker must then give the employer a completed Form W-4, and the tax withheld should be reported in box 2 of Form W-2. These reporting rules apply when the H-2A worker gives the employer their TIN. If the worker gives no TIN and total annual wages are at least $600 in 2025 ($2,000 in 2026), the employer is required to backup withhold; the page refers to the Instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC and the Instructions for Form 945. The page points to IRS.gov/H2A for more information.

**Employee business expense reimbursements (p. 19).** A reimbursement or allowance arrangement is a system for paying advances, reimbursements and charges for your employees' business expenses. How a reimbursement or allowance amount is reported depends on whether the plan is accountable or nonaccountable. When one payment covers both wages and an expense reimbursement, you must specify the reimbursement amount. These rules cover all allowable ordinary and necessary employee business expenses.

**Accountable plan (p. 19).** An arrangement is an accountable plan only if it requires employees to meet all three of these rules:
1. They paid or incurred allowable expenses while performing services as your employees, and the reimbursement or advance pays for those expenses rather than an amount that would otherwise have been paid to the employee as wages.
2. They substantiate the expenses to you within a reasonable period of time.
3. They return any amounts above the substantiated expenses within a reasonable period of time.

Amounts paid under an accountable plan are not wages and are not subject to income, social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes. Where expenses go unsubstantiated (or amounts above substantiated expenses are not returned within a reasonable period of time), the excess over the substantiated expenses is treated as paid under a nonaccountable plan. That excess is subject to income, social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes for the first payroll period after the reasonable period of time ends. What is a reasonable period depends on the facts and circumstances. Generally, these timings are considered reasonable: employees get their advance within 30 days of paying or incurring the expenses, account adequately for them within 60 days after they were paid or incurred, and return any excess within 120 days after they were paid or incurred. A periodic statement (at least quarterly) asking employees to return or adequately account for outstanding amounts is considered reasonable as an alternative, if they respond within 120 days.

**Nonaccountable plan (p. 19).** Payments to your employee for travel and other necessary business expenses under a nonaccountable plan are wages, are treated as supplemental wages, and are subject to income, social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes. Payments are treated as made under a nonaccountable plan in any of these cases:
- The employee isn't required to substantiate the expenses to you timely with receipts or other documentation, or doesn't do so.
- You advance an amount for business expenses and the employee isn't required to return timely whatever is unused for business expenses, or doesn't do so.
- You advance or pay an amount whether or not you reasonably expect the employee to have business expenses related to your business.
- You pay an amount as reimbursement that you would otherwise have paid as wages.

The page refers to section 7 for more information on supplemental wages.

**Per diem or other fixed allowance (pp. 19–20).** You may reimburse employees by travel days, miles or some other fixed allowance under the applicable revenue procedure. Your employee is then considered to have accounted to you if your reimbursement stays within the rates established by the federal government. Pub. 15-B gives the standard mileage rate for auto expenses. The government per diem rates for meals and lodging in the continental United States are on the U.S. General Services Administration website at GSA.gov/PerDiemRates. Apart from the amount of these expenses, employees' business expenses must be substantiated (for example, the business purpose of the travel or the number of business miles driven); for substantiation methods the page refers to Pub. 463, Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses. A per diem or allowance that exceeds the amounts substantiated must be reported as wages for the excess, and that excess is subject to income tax withholding and to social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes. The amount equal to the substantiated amount (the nontaxable portion) is shown in box 12 of Form W-2 with code "L." Employers in Puerto Rico report the amount in box 12 with no code.

**Wages not paid in money (p. 20).** If, in the course of your trade or business, you pay employees in a medium that is neither cash nor a readily negotiable instrument such as a check, you are said to pay them "in kind." Goods, lodging, food, clothing or services may be the form of payment in kind. Generally, the FMV of such payments when they are provided is subject to federal income tax withholding and social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes. Noncash payments for household work, agricultural labor and service not in the employer's trade or business, however, are exempt from social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes, and income tax is withheld on them only if you and the employee agree. Even so, where the substance of the transaction is a cash payment, noncash payments for agricultural labor (commodity wages, for example) are treated as cash payments subject to employment taxes. The page refers to Noncash wages, including commodity wages, paid to farmworkers, earlier in the section.

**Meals and lodging (p. 20).** Meals furnished for the employer's convenience and on the employer's premises are not taxable income and are not subject to federal income tax withholding or social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes. Lodging escapes federal income tax withholding and social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes when it is provided for the employer's convenience, on its premises and as a condition of employment. "For the convenience of the employer" means that you have a substantial business reason, other than giving the employee additional compensation, for providing the meals and lodging. For example, meals provided at the place of work to keep an employee available for emergencies during their lunch period are generally considered to be for your convenience. You must be able to show that such emergency calls have occurred or can reasonably be expected to occur, and that they have led, or will lead, to your calling on employees to do their jobs during the meal period. Whether meals or lodging are for the employer's convenience depends on all of the facts and circumstances, and a written statement that they are for your convenience isn't sufficient.

**50% test (p. 20).** When over 50% of the employees provided meals on an employer's business premises receive them for the employer's convenience, all meals provided on the premises are treated as furnished for the employer's convenience. When the 50% test is met, the meals' value can be excluded from all employees' income and is free of federal income tax withholding and employment taxes. The page refers to Pub. 15-B for more information.

**Health insurance plans (p. 20).** Your payments for an accident or health insurance plan for your employees, including an employee's spouse and dependents, aren't wages and aren't subject to social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes or to federal income tax withholding. Generally, the exclusion applies to qualified long-term-care insurance contracts too. For income tax withholding, though, wages of S corporation employees owning more than 2% of the S corporation (2% shareholders) must include the value of health insurance benefits. When it comes to social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes, a 2% shareholder's wages exclude those benefits. The page refers to Announcement 92-16 (page 53 of Internal Revenue Bulletin 1992-5).

**Health savings accounts (HSAs) and medical savings accounts (MSAs) (p. 20).** Your contributions to an employee's HSA or Archer MSA are free of social security, Medicare and FUTA tax and of federal income tax withholding if it is reasonable to believe, when you make them, that they will be excludable from the employee's income. To the extent that belief isn't reasonable, the contributions are subject to those taxes. Employee contributions made to their HSAs or MSAs through a payroll deduction plan count as wages; social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes apply to them, as does federal income tax withholding. HSA contributions made under a salary reduction arrangement in a section 125 cafeteria plan, however, aren't wages and aren't subject to employment taxes or withholding. The page refers to the Instructions for Form 8889.

**Medical care reimbursements (p. 20).** Generally, medical care reimbursements made for an employee under an employer's self-insured medical reimbursement plan are not wages, and social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes and federal income tax withholding don't apply to them. For a rule on including certain reimbursements in the gross income of highly compensated individuals, the page refers to Pub. 15-B.

**Differential wage payments (pp. 20–21).** Differential wage payments are payments by an employer to an individual for a period of more than 30 days of active duty service in the uniformed services, representing all or part of the wages the individual would have received from the employer if performing services for the employer. They are wages for income tax withholding but aren't subject to social security, Medicare or FUTA tax. Employers should report them in box 1 of Form W-2 (box 7 of Form 499R-2/W-2PR) (p. 21). The page refers to Revenue Ruling 2009-11 for more information on their tax treatment.

**Fringe benefits (p. 21).** Fringe benefits must generally be included in an employee's wages (the page refers to Nontaxable fringe benefits, next), and they are subject to income tax withholding and employment taxes. Examples include tickets to entertainment or sporting events, vacations, memberships in country clubs or other social clubs, cars you provide, flights on aircraft you provide, free or discounted commercial flights, and discounts on property or services. In general, the amount to include is the excess of the benefit's FMV over the sum of what the employee paid for it and any amount the law excludes. You and your employees may use other special rules to value certain fringe benefits. The page refers to Pub. 15-B for more information.

**Valuation of fringe benefits (p. 21).** Generally, the value of fringe benefits must be determined by January 31 of the next year at the latest. Until January 31 you may make a reasonable estimate of their value for purposes of withholding and depositing on time.

**When taxable fringe benefits are treated as paid (p. 21).** You may choose to treat certain taxable noncash fringe benefits as paid by the pay period, by the quarter or on any other basis, provided you treat the benefits as paid at least once a year. No formal choice of payment dates is needed, and you needn't notify the IRS of the dates. The choice need not cover all employees. You may change methods as often as you like, as long as all benefits provided in a calendar year are treated as paid by December 31 of that year. The page refers to section 4 of Pub. 15-B, including its discussion of the special accounting rule for fringe benefits provided during November and December.

**Nontaxable fringe benefits (p. 21).** Some fringe benefits aren't taxable (or are minimally taxable) if certain conditions are met; the page refers to Pub. 15-B for details. The page's examples:
- Services provided to your employees at no additional cost to you.
- Qualified employee discounts.
- Working condition fringes: property or services that the employee could have deducted as a business expense or depreciation expense had they paid for them, such as a company car for business use and subscriptions to business magazines.
- Certain minimal value fringes (an occasional cab ride when an employee must work overtime is one; so are meals you provide at eating places you run for your employees if the meals aren't furnished at below cost).
- Qualified transportation fringes within specified conditions and dollar limitations (including transportation in a commuter highway vehicle, any transit pass and qualified parking).
- Use of on-premises athletic facilities that you operate, if substantially all of the use is by employees, their spouses and their dependent children.
- Qualified tuition reduction that an educational organization provides to its employees for education; the page refers to Pub. 970.
- Employer-provided cell phones provided primarily for a noncompensatory business reason.

The following fringe benefits must not be excluded from the wages of highly compensated employees unless the benefit is available to other employees on a nondiscriminatory basis: reduced tuition for education, meals provided at an employer-operated eating facility, qualified employee discounts and no-additional-cost services. The page refers to Pub. 15-B for more information, including the definition of a highly compensated employee.

**Withholding federal income tax on fringe benefits (p. 21).** You may add the value of fringe benefits to regular wages for a payroll period and figure withholding taxes on the total. Alternatively, you may withhold federal income tax on the value of the fringe benefits at the optional flat 22% supplemental wage rate; for an employee who receives more than $1 million of supplemental wages during the calendar year, the page refers to Withholding on supplemental wages when an employee receives more than $1 million of supplemental wages during the calendar year in section 7. Withholding income tax on the value of an employee's personal use of a vehicle you provide is something you may choose not to do, though social security and Medicare taxes on that use must still be withheld. The page refers to Pub. 15-B for more information on this election.

**Withholding social security and Medicare taxes on fringe benefits (p. 21).** Social security and Medicare taxes are figured on the total of regular wages for a payroll period plus the value of fringe benefits. You may recover the taxes from the employee when, in a calendar year, you withhold less than the required social security and Medicare taxes but report and pay the proper amount. The page refers to Pub. 15-B for more information.

**Depositing taxes on fringe benefits (pp. 21–22).** After you choose when fringe benefits are paid, the taxes must be deposited in the same deposit period in which you treat the benefits as paid. To avoid a penalty, deposit them following the general deposit rules for that deposit period (p. 21). Suppose you determine by January 31 that you overestimated a fringe benefit's value when you withheld and deposited for it: you may claim a refund of the overpayment or have it applied to your next employment tax return, and the page refers to Valuation of fringe benefits, earlier in the section. If you underestimated the value and deposited too little, a failure-to-deposit (FTD) penalty may apply, and the page refers to section 11 for information on deposit penalties. When you deposited the required amount but withheld less from the employee, you can recover from the employee the social security, Medicare or income tax you deposited for them and included in their Form W-2, but the income tax must be recovered before April 1 of the following year (p. 22).

**Back pay (p. 22).** Back pay, retroactive wage increases included but amounts paid as liquidated damages excluded, is taxed as ordinary wages in the year it is paid. For reporting back pay to the SSA, the page refers to Pub. 957.

**Sick pay (p. 22).** In general, sick pay is any amount paid under a plan to an employee who can't work because of sickness or injury. A third party, such as an insurance company or an employees' trust, is sometimes the payer. Either way, the payments are subject to social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes. Sick pay paid more than 6 calendar months after the last calendar month in which the employee worked for the employer is outside those taxes; it is always subject to federal income tax. The page refers to section 6 of Pub. 15-A for more information.

**Overtime compensation (p. 22).** Under the FLSA, employers must generally pay covered, nonexempt employees at least one-and-a-half times the regular rate of pay for hours over 40 per week. The page points to dol.gov/agencies/whd/overtime for more information. Overtime compensation is subject to social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes and to federal income tax withholding. For tax years beginning after 2024 and ending before 2029, however, P.L. 119-21 lets individuals (employees and other workers not treated as employees) deduct up to $12,500 ($25,000 if married filing jointly) of qualified overtime compensation on their income tax returns. Qualified overtime is compensation above the regular rate of pay (such as the "half" portion of time-and-a-half compensation) that must be paid to an individual under section 7 of the FLSA of 1938. Where an employee submits an updated Form W-4, employers must use it, along with the federal income tax withholding procedures in Pub. 15-T, so the employee can account for the expected deduction and get more money in each paycheck rather than waiting until filing an income tax return to receive the deduction's full benefit.

**Identity protection services (p. 22).** The value of identity protection services that an employer provides to an employee is not part of the employee's gross income and need not be reported on an information return (such as Form W-2) filed for the employee. Services provided before a data breach occurs are included. The exception doesn't cover cash received instead of identity protection services or proceeds received under an identity theft insurance policy. The page refers to Announcement 2015-22 and Announcement 2016-02.
