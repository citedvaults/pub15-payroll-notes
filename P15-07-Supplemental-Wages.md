## Note
**Caution (p. 23).** Employers in American Samoa, Guam, the CNMI, the USVI and Puerto Rico can disregard the references to federal income tax withholding, unless they have employees who are subject to U.S. income tax withholding. Such employers should contact their local tax department for information about income tax withholding.

**What supplemental wages are (p. 23).** Supplemental wages are wage payments to an employee that aren't regular wages. The list is not limited to these: bonuses, commissions, overtime pay (the page refers to Overtime compensation in section 5), payments for accumulated sick leave, severance pay, awards, prizes, back pay, reported tips (the page refers to Federal income tax withholding on tips in section 6), retroactive pay increases and payments for nondeductible moving expenses. Employers may choose to treat overtime pay and tips as regular wages rather than as supplemental wages. Taxable fringe benefits, and expense allowances paid under a nonaccountable plan, are other payments that fall under the supplemental wage rules. The way you withhold depends on whether the supplemental payment is identified as separate from regular wages. The page refers to Regulations section 31.3402(g)-1 for additional guidance and to Revenue Ruling 2008-29 (pp. 23–24).

**Over $1 million of supplemental wages in a calendar year (p. 24).** Special rules come into play to the extent that supplemental wages paid to any one employee during the calendar year exceed $1 million. When a supplemental wage payment, added to the employee's other supplemental wage payments for the calendar year, brings the total above $1 million, the excess is subject to withholding at 37% (or the highest rate of income tax for the year). That 37% rate is used regardless of the employee's Form W-4. Payments from all businesses under common control are counted when determining the supplemental wages paid to the employee in the year. The page refers to Treasury Decision 9276.

**Up to $1 million of supplemental wages in a calendar year (p. 24).** Where an employee's supplemental wages for the calendar year are $1 million or less, these rules decide the income tax to withhold:
- *Supplemental wages combined with regular wages.* If supplemental and regular wages are paid together without the amount of each being specified, withhold federal income tax as though the total were one payment for a regular payroll period.
- *Supplemental wages identified separately from regular wages.* Where supplemental wages are paid separately (or combined in one payment with the amount of each specified), the withholding method depends partly on whether you withhold income tax from the employee's regular wages.

If you withheld income tax from the employee's regular wages in the current or immediately preceding calendar year, you can choose between two methods for the supplemental wages:
1. Method 1a: a flat 22% (no other percentage is allowed).
2. Method 1b: when the supplemental wages are paid concurrently with regular wages, combine the two and withhold federal income tax as though the total were one payment for a regular payroll period. When no regular wages are paid concurrently, add the supplemental wages to either the regular wages paid or to be paid for the current payroll period, or the regular wages paid for the preceding payroll period, and figure the income tax withholding on that total as one payment. Subtract the tax already withheld or to be withheld from the regular wages, and withhold the remainder from the supplemental wages. If supplemental wages were paid earlier in the same payroll period, aggregate all supplemental payments for the period with the period's regular wages, figure the tax on the total, subtract the tax already withheld from the regular wages and from the earlier supplemental payments, and withhold what is left.

If you did not withhold income tax from the employee's regular wages in the current or immediately preceding calendar year, method 1b is the one to use. Whichever method is used for income tax withholding, supplemental wages are subject to social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes.

**Examples (pp. 24–25).**
- Example 1: John Peters gets a base salary on the first of each month. His most recent Form W-4 dates from 2018; he is single, claims one withholding allowance and entered no amount for additional withholding. For January he is paid $1,000 and, with the Wage Bracket Method, Worksheet 3 and the withholding tables in section 3 of Pub. 15-T, $2 is withheld. For February he receives his $1,000 salary plus a $500 commission that is combined with regular wages and not separately identified, so withholding is figured on the $1,500 total; the tables give $53 (p. 24).
- Example 2: Sharon Warren gets a base salary on the first of each month. She submitted a 2026 Form W-4 with the Single or Married filing separately box checked and Steps 2, 3 and 4 left incomplete. Her May 1 pay is $2,000; using the Wage Bracket Method, Worksheet 2 and the withholding tables in section 2 of Pub. 15-T, $65 is withheld. On May 15 she receives a $1,000 bonus, and the employer elects method 1b: add the bonus to the wages of the most recent base salary pay date, May 1 ($2,000 + $1,000 = $3,000); the wage bracket tables give $179 of withholding on that $3,000; subtract the $65 withheld on May 1 ($179 – $65 = $114); withhold $114 from the bonus (p. 24).
- Example 3: With the facts of Example 2 but the flat rate method elected for the bonus, 22% of $1,000, or $220, is withheld from the bonus (p. 24).
- Example 4: With the facts of Example 2 plus a second bonus of $2,000 on May 29, and method 1b used, the first and second bonuses are added to the wages of the most recent base salary pay date, May 1 ($2,000 + $1,000 + $2,000 = $5,000); the wage bracket tables give $419 of withholding on that $5,000; the amounts withheld on May 1 and from the first bonus are subtracted ($419 – $65 – $114 = $240); and $240 is withheld from the second bonus (pp. 24–25).

**Tips are treated as supplemental wages (p. 25).** Income tax on tips is withheld from wages the employee earns or from other funds the employee makes available (the page refers to Federal income tax withholding on tips in section 6), and not from the employee's tips. When an employee gets regular wages and reports tips, income tax withholding is figured as if the tips were supplemental wages. If income tax was withheld from the regular wages in the current or immediately preceding calendar year, you can use method 1a or 1b, covered earlier in the section under Supplemental wages identified separately from regular wages. If it was not, add the tips to the regular wages and withhold income tax on the total by method 1b. Treating tips as regular wages instead is an option employers have. Service charges aren't tips, so taxes on them are withheld as for regular wages.

**Vacation pay (p. 25).** Withholding applies to vacation pay as though it were a regular wage payment. Vacation pay that comes on top of regular wages for the vacation period (an annual lump-sum payment for unused vacation leave, for example) is treated as a supplemental wage payment. Vacation pay covering a time longer than the usual payroll period is spread over the pay periods for which it is paid.
