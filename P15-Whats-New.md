## Note
**Social security and Medicare taxes for 2026 (pp. 1–2).** For 2026 the employer and the employee each pay social security tax at 6.2% on taxable wages, up to a wage base limit of $184,500. Each also pays Medicare tax at 1.45%, a rate unchanged from 2025, and Medicare tax has no wage base limit. The two taxes apply to household workers when you pay them $3,000 or more in cash wages in 2026, and to election workers paid $2,500 or more in 2026 in cash or an equivalent form of compensation.

**Permanent extension of individual tax rates (p. 2).** Under P.L. 119-21, known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the individual income tax rates set by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (P.L. 115-97) are extended permanently. To figure federal income tax withholding, employers keep using Pub. 15-T, Federal Income Tax Withholding Methods.

**Withholding on supplemental wages (p. 2).** The rate stays at 22%, or 37% if the supplemental wages paid to an employee during the calendar year exceed $1 million. The stated reason is that P.L. 119-21 made the individual rates of P.L. 115-97 permanent. The page refers to section 7 for more on supplemental wages.

**Backup withholding (p. 2).** The backup withholding rate stays at 24%, for the same stated reason. For reportable payments under section 6041(a) or 6041A(a) that are made in calendar year 2026 and are subject to backup withholding, P.L. 119-21 lifts the aggregate reportable payment threshold from $600 to $2,000. An inflation adjustment of that threshold follows for each calendar year after 2026. The page refers to Backup withholding, later.

**Information reporting threshold (p. 2).** For payments made after calendar year 2025, P.L. 119-21 lifts the payment threshold for information reporting (for example, Forms 1099-MISC and Forms 1099-NEC) from $600 to $2,000 per calendar year. This covers certain payments to persons engaged in a trade or business, and payments for services. Wage reporting on Forms W-2 gets the same $2,000 threshold if no federal income, social security or Medicare tax was withheld. Inflation adjustments follow for each calendar year after 2026. The page refers to the General Instructions for Forms W-2 and W-3 and to the General Instructions for Certain Information Returns.

**Withholding on qualified tips (p. 2).** For tax years beginning after 2024 and ending before 2029, P.L. 119-21 permits employees and self-employed individuals to deduct up to $25,000 of qualified tips on their income tax returns, provided the tips came from occupations that customarily and regularly received tips on or before December 31, 2024. Qualified tips are cash tips: voluntary cash or charged tips from customers or, for employees, tips received through tip-sharing arrangements. Mandatory service charges added to the bill do not qualify. If an employee submits an updated Form W-4, the employer must use it with the withholding procedures in Pub. 15-T, so that the employee can reflect the expected deduction and receive more money in each paycheck, rather than waiting to get the full benefit when filing the income tax return. Tips of $20 or more per month are still generally subject to both shares of social security and Medicare tax. Information returns (for example, Forms W-2, 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC) must be filed by employers and other payers with the SSA or the IRS, as applicable. Tip recipients must also be given statements showing the cash tips received and the recipient’s Treasury Tipped Occupation Code(s). The IRS has granted transition relief for the tax year 2025 reporting requirements; the page refers to Notice 2025-62, 2025-48 I.R.B. 740.

**Withholding on qualified overtime compensation (p. 2).** For tax years beginning after 2024 and ending before 2029, P.L. 119-21 permits individuals (employees, and other workers not treated as employees) to deduct up to $12,500 of qualified overtime compensation, or $25,000 if married filing jointly. The deduction comes out of income subject to federal income tax and is claimed on income tax returns. Qualified overtime is pay above the regular rate that section 7 of the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) of 1938 requires an individual to be paid, such as the “half” portion of time-and-a-half. Under the FLSA, covered, nonexempt employees must generally be paid at least one-and-a-half times their regular rate for hours over 40 in a week; the page points to dol.gov/agencies/whd/overtime. If an employee submits an updated Form W-4, the employer must use it with the Pub. 15-T procedures, so that the employee can account for the expected deduction in each paycheck. Overtime pay is still generally subject to the employer and employee shares of social security and Medicare tax. Employers and other payers file information returns (for example, Forms W-2, 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC) with the SSA or the IRS, as applicable, and must also furnish overtime recipients with statements of the qualified overtime compensation paid during the year. Transition relief applies to the tax year 2025 reporting requirements, and the page refers to Notice 2025-62.

**Moving expense reimbursement (pp. 2–3).** P.L. 119-21 permanently ends the exclusion of qualified moving expense reimbursements from an employee’s income. The exclusion is available in two cases. One is an active-duty member of the U.S. Armed Forces who moves because of a permanent change of station under a military order; for that member the exclusion covers only reimbursement of moving expenses deductible had they been paid or incurred without reimbursement, and the page refers to Moving Expenses in Pub. 3, Armed Forces’ Tax Guide. The other, added by P.L. 119-21, is an employee or new appointee in the intelligence community (the term is defined in section 3 of the National Security Act of 1947, 50 U.S.C. 3003) who is not a member of the Armed Forces and moves under a change in assignment requiring relocation; the exclusion again covers only reimbursement of moving expenses they could deduct had they paid or incurred them without reimbursement.

**Employer contributions to Trump accounts (p. 3).** A Trump account is a new type of traditional individual retirement account that P.L. 119-21 allows to be set up for a child who has not reached age 18 by the end of the year the account is established. Its annual contribution limit is $5,000 (other than exempt contributions), indexed for inflation after tax year 2027. Starting July 4, 2026, an employer may put up to $2,500 a year, indexed for inflation after tax year 2027, toward that limit in the Trump account of an employee or of an employee’s dependent. The amount is excluded from the employee’s gross income if paid under a Trump account contribution program. The page refers to Pub. 15-A, Employer’s Supplemental Tax Guide.

**Direct deposit of employment tax refunds (p. 3).** Executive Order (EO) 14247, Modernizing Payments To and From America’s Bank Account, was issued on March 25, 2025. It promotes operational efficiency by mandating electronic payments for all federal disbursements, and the IRS will now therefore issue employment tax return refunds by direct deposit to a checking or savings account. As an alternative to the direct deposit refund, you can still choose to have the overpayment go toward your next return, which you do by checking the appropriate box on your employment tax return. The page refers to the instructions for your employment tax return.

**Make balance due payments electronically (p. 3).** EO 14247 likewise mandates electronic payments for all payments to the federal government. The page therefore says to pay the balance due on your employment tax return electronically, and refers to the instructions for your employment tax return for the ways to do so.

**Employment tax return transcripts (p. 3).** Return transcripts of Forms 940, 941, 943, 944 and 945 for tax years 2023 and later are now available electronically through your IRS business tax account. The page points to IRS.gov/BusinessTranscript, and to IRS.gov/BusinessAccount for the business tax account.
